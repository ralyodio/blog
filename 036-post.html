Work is not disappearing. The job is.

The thing we call a job, meaning a salary, a fixed schedule, one employer for a decade or three, is not the natural shape of work. It is an industrial-age artifact. It made sense when the machinery was expensive and immobile and you had to gather people around it at the same hour. Almost none of that is true anymore, and AI agents plus humanoid robots are dismantling what is left of it from both ends: cognitive work by the agents, physical work by the robots.

Repetitive, risky and simple gets automated first. That is not a prediction so much as a description of every technology wave that came before this one. What is different is the pace, and the fact that this one arrived for office work and factory work at roughly the same time.

The schedule dies before the work does

The 9-to-5 goes first, because it is the part with the least justification. It survives on the belief that presence is a proxy for output, and that belief is losing.

You can watch it lose in the return-to-office numbers. Executives have been loudly mandating a return for four years now, and remote work has not collapsed. It flattened, at roughly a quarter of US working days, and it has stayed there. Most remote-capable employees want hybrid, fewer than one in ten want to be in the office five days a week, and around half say they would go look for another job if a full-time mandate landed on them. The mandates make headlines. The badge data keeps not matching the mandates.

That gap is the whole story in miniature. The org chart says one thing and the actual behaviour of the people in it says another, and the behaviour wins slowly and then all at once.

Missions, not tenure

What replaces employment is engagement. You do not hire a person for twenty years, you engage an expert for a mission, and you measure the thing in projects rather than in years served.

This one is further along than most people realise. MBO Partners counts roughly 73 million independent workers in the US, which is getting close to half the labour force. Read that number carefully though, because the honest version is less dramatic: about 28 million of those are full-time independents and the rest are doing it part-time or on the side. The shift is real and it is large, but it is happening as a slow migration rather than a mass resignation, and a lot of it is people holding a job with one hand while building the other thing with the other.

That is the sane way to do it, incidentally. Nobody should quit on a thesis.

Where I will not give you a number

I was going to put humanoid robot deployment figures here. I went looking, and what I found was a pile of confident unit counts that trace back to nobody. The companies they describe have not published audited production numbers, and the sites quoting them cite each other. So I am leaving the number out. The direction is obvious to anyone watching, and the specific counts currently circulating are marketing.

Be suspicious of anybody who quotes you one, including anybody making my argument.

The universities have the biggest problem

If you are selling a four-year degree as preparation for a job that will not exist in four years, you are selling a bond backed by an asset in decline, and the buyers are eighteen and taking on debt to buy it.

The usual counter is that employers are dropping degree requirements anyway, so the market is already correcting. It is not, and the research on this is brutal. Joseph Fuller's team at Harvard Business School looked at more than 11,000 job postings from 2014 to 2023 and found that when companies removed degree requirements, actual hiring barely moved. Fewer than 1 in 700 new hires benefited. Fuller's word for it is virtue washing.

Which is worse for the universities, not better. The degree is losing its value as a signal while still being demanded as a filter. Students pay for it, get less for it, and cannot skip it. That is a position that holds right up until it does not, and institutions in that position have historically not seen it coming.

What actually works

Three things, and none of them are clever.

Treat AI as an amplifier rather than a replacement or a threat. The people doing well with it right now are the ones who already knew how to do the work and now do six times as much of it. It does not turn you into an expert. It makes an expert faster, and it makes a novice produce confident garbage at scale.

Build a public reputation. When the unit of work is a mission rather than a tenure, the thing you are selling is evidence that you can do the mission. A repository, a body of writing, shipped things with your name on them. This is what replaces the résumé, and it is worth more than the résumé ever was because it cannot be claimed, only demonstrated.

Never stop learning, which sounds like a fortune cookie until you notice that the half-life on specific technical knowledge is now shorter than a degree programme.

The people who do those three things will be fine. The institutions betting that none of this is happening will not, and they will have had a decade of warning.
