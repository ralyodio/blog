What happened

Someone I don't know — buffer_overlord — submitted my post about the 123 security findings to Hacker News. I didn't post it and didn't know it was there until later. It did not take off. Two points and one comment, which on that site is indistinguishable from never having been posted at all.

The one comment was worth more than a front page would have been. I want to write it down before the thread scrolls off into wherever dead HN submissions go.

The comment

jeffreygoesto quoted the bit at the end of my post about second-order lessons, and then described what their team actually does:

We are taking turns in small teams being on duty for only finding and fixing bugs for two weeks. They are working on features with a much sharpened eye after and don't repeat bad habits so easily. Having drilled down to the core gives you the necessary understanding.

I have been turning that over since I read it, because it is the generalized version of the thing I paid an outside engineer to do to me once.

Why it lands

The conclusion I reached in the audit post was that most of the value wasn't in the findings. It was in what the remediation taught me — that my REVOKE statements were decorative, that a passing CI check only means no new alerts, that quiet database logs are not evidence of a harmless schema mismatch. None of that is in an advisory. You learn it by doing the fix and then checking whether the fix actually took.

Here is the uncomfortable part I glossed over: that knowledge went into exactly one head. Mine. I did the remediation, so I got the education. Everyone else on the project got a merged PR and a green checkmark, which teaches nothing.

A rotation fixes that, and it fixes it in the one way that works — by making people do the boring part themselves rather than reading someone else's summary of it. You cannot transfer “the catalogs disagree with the migration output” in a postmortem. You can only transfer it by having somebody sit there and query the catalog and feel the small shock of the mismatch.

The second thing that comment gets right is the direction of the effect. I would have assumed the payoff of bug duty is the bugs you fix. jeffreygoesto's claim is that the payoff is what happens after — people build features differently once they've spent two weeks living in the wreckage of how features get built. Bugs fixed is the visible output. Habits changed is the actual product.

What I'm not sure about

I don't want to write an enthusiastic post about a practice I haven't run, so, honestly:

  • The cost is real and it is not hidden. A team on bug duty is a team not shipping. If you have four small teams, you are permanently running at three quarters capacity on features. I suspect that trade is correct and I am certain it is unpopular.
  • I don't know how it degrades solo. Most of what I do is one or two people deep. A rotation with a rotation size of one is just “spend two weeks on bugs,” which loses the part where a fresh pair of eyes inherits somebody else's mess. That inheritance might be the whole mechanism.
  • Two weeks may be load-bearing. Long enough that you run out of easy bugs and have to go find real ones. A two-day version is probably just triage with extra steps.

What I'm going to do

The audit was a one-time purchase of paranoia. It worked, and I am not going to buy one every quarter. The rotation is the same mechanism made cheap and repeating, and the honest reason I hadn't considered it is that I think of security work as something that arrives from outside on an invoice.

So I'm going to try the smallest version that keeps the important property: whoever is on duty works on a repo they did not write. Not a two-week block — I can't afford that shape yet — but a standing slot, on somebody else's code, with the rule that anything found gets fixed by the finder rather than filed for the author. If it produces even one lesson of the “your REVOKE statements are decorative” variety, it beats the audit on price by a wide margin.

Thanks, jeffreygoesto. And thanks to whoever posted it — two points was worth it.
