The Antikythera mechanism was an astronomy app with a crank

2026-10-01, by Anthony “chovy” Ettinger.

How this was written: drafted with an AI assistant from my own notes, then edited by me.

In 1901, sponge divers working an ancient wreck off the Greek island of Antikythera pulled up a lump of corroded bronze. It turned out to be a geared calculator, built somewhere around the 2nd or 1st century BC.

You picked a date by turning a handle. Gears moved the dials, and the dials showed you what the sky would look like on that day, plus when an eclipse might happen. It was an astronomy app. The code was made of bronze teeth.

What it showed you

OutputWhat it meant
Sun and Moon positionsWhere each would sit along the zodiac, the band of sky they move through.
Moon phaseNew, full, crescent, and so on.
Eclipse predictionsMonths when a solar or lunar eclipse could occur, with an estimated hour inscribed on the dial.
Calendar cyclesHow lunar months lined up with solar years, using a 19-year cycle of 235 lunar months.
Games scheduleWhich year in the cycle had the Olympics and the other Greek games.

Each of these is backed by surviving gears, dials, or inscriptions (PMC).

The algorithm is the hardware

A gear ratio is a multiplication. If a 60-tooth gear drives a 20-tooth gear, the small one turns three times for every turn of the big one. Chain enough ratios together and "days advanced" becomes "where the Moon is" or "how far we are through the eclipse cycle."

The cleverest part is the Moon. The real Moon doesn't move across the sky at a constant speed. It runs faster when it's closer to Earth and slower when it's farther away. The mechanism handled this with two gears on slightly offset axles, coupled by a pin riding in a slot. The output wheel speeds up and slows down once a month, which is roughly what the Moon does (Nature, 2006). Nobody wrote a formula for that. They cut it into metal.

What it couldn't do

The front of the device is mostly gone. It probably showed the five planets known at the time (Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn), and in 2021 a UCL team published a full reconstruction of how that might have worked (UCL News). But the planetary gearing is inferred, not recovered, so treat it as a strong guess.

The eclipse predictions were also approximate. The dial could tell you a solar eclipse was possible in a given month. It couldn't tell you whether it would be visible from your town.

Why I keep coming back to it

Most of what we call software is a model of something, run on general-purpose hardware. This is the other way around: a model of the sky with no separation between program and machine. Change the prediction and you have to recut the gears.

It's also a reminder that the people who built it had good data. You don't get a 235-month cycle or eclipse timings from a guess. You get them from centuries of Babylonian and Greek observation, written down, checked, and finally turned into something you could hold in your hands and crank.
