Four genres, and the filter that was eating the other 89%

2026-08-21, by Anthony “chovy” Ettinger.

How this was written: drafted with an AI assistant from my own notes, then edited by me.

back to my blog postings

The music section of one of my sites listed four genres. Alternative Rock, Emo, Rock, Singer-Songwriter. One release coming in each. It had looked like that for a while and I had a story about why: MusicBrainz is a volunteer database, upcoming releases are thinly covered, nothing to be done.

That story was wrong, and the evidence had been sitting in my own code the whole time.

The number that matters

MusicBrainz knows about 2,228 official releases in a four month forward window. My ingest kept 245 of them.

The filter was one line: 

if (!r.date || r.date.length !== 10) continue;

Ten characters means 2026-09-18. Anything shorter got dropped. And it turns out only 11% of upcoming releases carry a full date, because a label announces a quarter long before it announces a day. The other 89% arrive as 2026-09.

So I was throwing away nine out of every ten releases, and with them most of the genres, because a genre only appears on the site once something in it is coming. Four genres was not a thin source. It was a thin filter, applied to a source that was doing fine.

Precision is data

The original decision had a good instinct behind it. A comment above the filter said a reminder site that invents a release date is worse than one that admits it does not know. I still agree with that. But it conflated two different things.

Storing 2026-09 as September 18th would be inventing a date. Storing it as September, and saying September, is reporting exactly what you were told. The first is a lie. The second is just a coarser truth, and coarser truth is what most of the music industry publishes ten months a year.

The fix was to keep month precision and render it as "September 2026" instead of a day nobody claimed. A bare year still gets dropped, because "sometime in 2026" is not something a reader can act on and it would swamp a four month window with a whole year.

The infrastructure was already there

Here is the part that stung. I went looking for how much work this would be, and found:

  • a precision column on the events table, present since the very first migration
  • another adapter, the spaceflight one, already emitting month and doing so from the day it was written
  • a rendering helper that already formatted month and year values correctly

Every piece of it existed and was in use. The music adapter was the only one not participating. The change was one function and about fifteen lines.

The free half

While I was in there I found a second thing. Genres come from an artist lookup that costs one request, and MusicBrainz rate limits to one request per second, so those requests are the expensive part of the whole job. The response comes back with every genre tag the community has voted on. The code kept the top four and discarded the rest.

Those requests were already paid for. An artist reads "rock, alternative rock, indie rock, shoegaze, dream pop" and I was keeping the first four, which are the generic ones, and binning the fifth, which is the one somebody would actually want to browse by. Changing a 4 to an 8 cost nothing and added the interesting half back.

Where it landed

Four genres became forty three. Jazz, shoegaze, post-punk, synthwave, symphonic metal, minimal wave, trap, punk blues, spoken word, noise, power metal, and thirty more. Same source, same API key, same rate limit.

Two things slowed it down after the code was right, and both are worth knowing if you run anything similar. The music sync only runs every twelve hours, so the fix sat there doing nothing until I forced a pass. And the artist lookup budget was tuned for the old volume, so I raised it, and then the job hit its wall clock deadline before it could spend the new budget. Coverage is still filling in on its own schedule.

The general version

Before you conclude a data source is thin, go and measure what your own ingest discards. The source is easy to blame because you cannot see inside it, and your filter is easy to trust because you wrote it and it looked reasonable at the time.

Mine looked reasonable. It was also silently rejecting 89% of everything it was handed, and had been since the day I wrote it.
