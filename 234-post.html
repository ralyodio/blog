Should you move back to SF to get a tech job?

Someone asked me this the other day. They worked in San Francisco during the 90s dotcom run, in their 20s. They're in their 50s now, they've been laid off for three years, and every job board they try is a black hole. Their question: do I have to move back to the city?

My honest answer is maybe, but not first.

The boards aren't a real channel anymore

If you've sent hundreds of applications and heard nothing, it isn't only you. Every open role gets buried in applications, a lot of them written and sent by bots. Recruiters filter on keywords and recency. A gap of three years and a resume that starts in 1997 lose that filter before a person ever reads them.

So stop treating applications as the main effort. Keep sending a few, but put most of your hours somewhere else.

Where the jobs actually come from

People get hired through people. That was true in 1999 and it's more true now. Here is where I would look:

  • Hacker News "Who is hiring?" A thread goes up on the first weekday of every month. Founders post in it themselves, and a short, direct email to the address in the post gets read far more often than an ATS form does.
  • Work at a Startup (workatastartup.com). That's Y Combinator's job board. The companies are small and the founders read their own inboxes.
  • Career pages, directly. Pick 30 companies whose products you use or respect. Find the engineering lead on LinkedIn or GitHub and write two sentences about something specific they shipped.
  • Your old network. The people you worked with in the 90s are directors, VPs and founders now. Many would be glad to hear from you. "I'm looking, here's what I'm working on" is a fine email to send.

The thing that matters more than the zip code

Software engineering isn't dead. It changed. The engineers getting hired right now ship fast with AI coding tools: they run agents, review what the agents produce, and know when it's wrong. Twenty-five years of experience is an advantage there, because judging code is the hard part now and typing it isn't.

But nobody will assume you can do it. Show them. Build something small and real with these tools, put it on GitHub, write up what broke along the way. A project from last month beats a resume line from 2004.

So, move?

SF is where the AI hiring is concentrated, and a lot of those companies want people in the office. Being there gets you to meetups, demo nights and hackathons, and that's where the hallway conversations that turn into offers happen. Look at lu.ma for SF tech events and you'll find several every night.

But moving is expensive and you can't easily undo it, and you don't know yet whether it will work. Try it small first. Sublet a room for two or three weeks. Go to an event every night, have coffee with anyone who'll say yes, and talk to founders face to face. If you come home with real leads, you have your answer. If you don't, you've spent a few thousand dollars and not your savings.

Ageism exists. I won't pretend it doesn't. What gets past it best is being visibly current: recent work, recent tools, recent conversations. You can start all of that this week from wherever you live.
