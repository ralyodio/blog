The break and fix is the part worth watching

How this was written: drafted with an AI assistant from my own notes, then edited by me.

Somebody on Bluesky wrote that polished tutorials hide the friction, and that the break and fix is where the real system logic gets built. I replied that this is exactly why I like the job again. The agent handles the implementation and the syntax, which is the part I hated in hindsight, and what is left is the part worth watching.

Here is one from this morning, still warm.

The bug

I asked for a CrawlProof ad unit on this blog. It went in, it rendered, it looked great in the screenshot. Then I looked at the actual page. This blog has no CSS. It is black text on a white background, on purpose. The ad was a 40px bar of near black, sitting at the bottom of a white page like a hole punched in it.

Nothing errored. The tests passed. The unit was doing exactly what it was told, which was to render itself in the only palette any CrawlProof creative had ever had. Every ad in the network was generated by a prompt that asked for one colour trio, and the model, given a free hand, picked dark every time. That is invisible until somebody runs the tag on a white page.

The fix, and the part I did not expect

The obvious move is a setting. Add data-theme="light" to the snippet and let the publisher deal with it. That fixes my blog and nobody else's, because nobody else knows the setting exists.

So every creative now carries two palettes instead of one, and the tag works out which to ask for. It walks up from the ad container looking for the first element with a real background colour, converts it to relative luminance, and sends light or dark with the fill request. If nothing on the page paints a background at all, which is my blog exactly, the answer is light, because that is what the browser actually paints. Not what the operating system prefers. A page with no CSS is white on a dark desktop too.

The generator asks the model for both trios now. It does not trust either one: each is checked for real contrast against the background it will sit on, and a trio that fails gets replaced by one derived from the other. Same hue, moved in lightness until it clears 4.5 to 1 for text and 3 to 1 for the button. An accent picked to glow on near black is almost always too pale to read on near white, and that is not something you notice by looking at the dark version.

Then the interesting problem. The stored colours were documented as the dark palette but that was never enforced, so some advertiser could always have picked white. The backfill measures instead of assuming: if a creative's stored background is actually light, it gets moved into the light columns and a dark one is derived, rather than being handed a light variant that is already light. Slots get a default the same way, by reading the publisher's own site and deciding from its declared body background.

What the tutorial would have shown

A tutorial would have shown the finished snippet and the two nice screenshots. It would not have shown that the first version shipped a black bar onto a white page, or that theme-color is useless for this because it is routinely a dark brand colour on a white site, or that auto refilling the ad when the theme changes would bill advertisers for impressions nobody newly saw, so the tag deliberately does not do it.

None of that is in the docs. All of it came out of breaking it in public.
