Who's writing this

I'm Anthony Ettinger. Most of the internet knows me as chovy, or as ralyodio on GitHub, where I've been pushing code since 2008 and have somewhere north of 250 public repos. I run Profullstack, Inc. out of Los Gatos. These days I build most of it with agents — I don't type a lot of the code anymore, I review it, and that's what this blog is going to be about.

But the first post isn't about agents. It's about a stock, a waiting room, and who actually gets paid.

The dentist's office

I was at the dentist. Got to talking with one of the women who works there. Somewhere in the small talk it came out that before this she'd been a machinist — years on a mill, holding tolerances, making parts that ended up inside surgical lasers. Cataract systems. The machines an ophthalmologist points at your eye.

She talked about it the way people talk about work they were good at. Tolerances, fixturing, what the parts were for. Then she talked about why she isn't doing it anymore.

What I did with that

I went home and pulled the ticker up in advis0r, which is a research tool I built. LNSR — LENSAR, Inc., NASDAQ. Here's what my own tool said, roughly at the time I looked:

  • Last price $8.25 (2026-08-13 close, delayed)
  • Overall score 49.53 — classified conservative
  • RSI-14 at 83. That is not a quiet stock; that's overbought
  • Relative volume 9.2× normal, on a +19.7% gap
  • Still -36% below its 52-week high, and +66% above its 52-week low

Read that honestly and it is not a buy signal. A sub-50 score with an RSI of 83 is the machine telling you something just moved and you are late. The breakout flag was on, sure, but so was the death cross. My own software did not tell me to buy this.

I bought it anyway. I'm up about 30%.

I want to be precise about what happened there, because it would be easy and flattering to tell it the other way. I did not out-analyze anyone. I had a conversation with a person who had physically touched the product, in a waiting room, by accident. That was the whole edge.

The part that actually bothers me

She made the parts. She got a wage.

I'm fairly confident nobody ever handed her equity, and if they had it wouldn't have been enough to notice. That's not a scandal, it's just the structure: on a cap table, the people closest to the physical product are an expense line. Cost of goods sold. Equity goes to the people who write the cap table and the people who fund it. Machinists get hourly plus, if the year is good, a raise that trails the CPI.

So here's the arithmetic that stuck with me. She spent years making the thing. I overheard a story about the thing and made 30% in a few weeks without leaving my chair. Neither of those numbers reflects effort. They reflect what kind of claim you hold — wage claim or ownership claim. One of those compounds and one of them doesn't.

You can call that blue-collar BS, and it is. But it's not bad luck, it's a design decision, made over and over, by people like me. Anybody who has ever issued a stock option has decided where the line goes. Every founder reading this drew it somewhere this year.

Why this ends up on a tech blog

Because it's the same trade I'm watching happen in software right now, and faster.

I build with agents all day. The leverage is real and it is enormous — I ship things in an afternoon that used to be a quarter of work for a team. But leverage doesn't distribute itself. It accrues to whoever holds the claim. The person doing the careful, skilled, hands-on work is gradually reclassified as a cost, and the person holding the tooling and the equity captures the delta. That's the machinist's story with a different job title.

I don't have a tidy fix. I do think the least I can do is not pretend the 30% was skill.
