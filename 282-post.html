The patch was the outage: what the NSA network going down teaches the rest of us

2026-11-25, by Anthony “chovy” Ettinger.

How this was written: drafted with an AI assistant from the CBS News report and my own notes, then edited by me.

Last Tuesday the NSA’s internal network went down. This is the classified network the agency uses to store and share information. CBS News reports that it wasn’t an attack. It was the fix.

Several sources told CBS the outage came from urgent patching for PHP2Shell, a set of vulnerabilities in PHP that were found with help from AI. On October 21 the FBI, NSA and CISA put out a joint warning: patch now, and if you can’t, take the system offline until you can. The NSA patched, and some of its systems didn’t come back up.

What happened

  • The network went offline on Tuesday. By Wednesday two sources said NSA Net was “back up and running.”
  • Staff at Fort Meade came in on staggered times, a schedule called “Code Gray”, because people feared the system couldn’t handle everyone logging in at once. CBS said nobody knew yet how long that would last.
  • Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton said on Fox Business that the patching had “caused some of these systems to not come back up when you patch them as expected. That is what has happened at the NSA today.” He also said, “This type of identification of vulnerabilities and patching goes on all the time.”
  • The NSA’s own statement called it a “temporary degradation during scheduled routine maintenance” and said mission-critical capabilities stayed fully operational.
  • An unnamed intelligence official told CBS that the number of changes, and how fast they come, can overwhelm old equipment built for an earlier internet.

The same report mentions the FAA’s nationwide ground stop the Friday before. That was hardware: routine telecom maintenance sent so much traffic through older routers that they fell over. It isn’t clear yet whether the banking, airline and tech outages of the last week share a cause.

The patch was the outage

“Patch or shut it off” sounds like a choice between being safe and staying up. It isn’t. Patching is also a change to production, and production changes break things. Most people only count the risk of not patching. The NSA found out about the other one in public.

This is going to keep happening, and AI is the reason. PHP2Shell was found with help from AI, in a language that CBS says runs about 70% of websites. Models are good at reading code nobody has read properly in years, and the old corners of big projects are full of it. I wrote last time about agents reading minified code faster than you can open DevTools. The same skill finds bugs. So expect more urgent advisories, closer together, against software you forgot you were running.

That shifts the bottleneck. Finding bugs is getting cheap, and shipping the fix safely isn’t. If a patch takes your systems down every time, you’ll start putting patches off, and that’s worse.

What I’d take from it

  • Know where the vulnerable thing runs before the advisory comes out. “Do we run PHP?” shouldn’t take a day to answer. Keep an inventory, or at least something you can search, of every box, container and dependency.
  • Roll out in stages. Patch one machine, check it comes back and actually serves traffic, then do the next batch. We deploy across our fleet three at a time for exactly this reason. Everything at once means everything breaks at once.
  • Test the restart, not just the patch. “Didn’t come back up” is usually a boot or startup problem, not a code bug. Services that were running fine for two years have never actually been restarted in that time, and that’s where the surprises are. Reboot on purpose, on a quiet day, so a patch day isn’t the first time.
  • Plan for everyone logging in at once. Code Gray is the NSA working around a thundering herd. After an outage, every user and every client retries at the same moment. Use backoff, jitter and queues, and rate-limit the login path.
  • Take the old hardware seriously. The FAA routers and the NSA’s “aging equipment” have the same problem. They were sized for a world with fewer changes and less traffic. If you can’t replace something, at least know where it is, so it isn’t the first thing that falls over.
  • Know the fallback before you need it. CISA’s advice was to take a system offline if you can’t patch it. That only works if you already know what turning it off breaks.

We’ve had our own bad week this month. I wrote up how an attacker got a shell through our Postgres. Most of the cleanup was the boring stuff on this list: finding everything, changing one thing at a time, and checking each one came back. None of it is exciting. It’s the difference between a patch and an outage.

When an urgent patch lands, how do you roll it out: all at once, in stages, or do you wait and see who breaks first?
